# Ethics of Asymmetric Contact and Its Relevance to Human–AGI Futures

## Assumptions and scope

This report treats “uncontacted” and “low-contact” communities as **Indigenous Peoples in Voluntary Isolation and Initial Contact** (often abbreviated as IPVIIC) and focuses on norms and protocols designed to prevent (or minimize) harm in **radically asymmetric** encounters—where one side has overwhelming advantages (technology, population scale, institutions, weapons, pathogens, extractive capacity). citeturn36view0turn19view0

The human–AGI analogy is used as an **analysis tool**, not an equivalence claim: Indigenous peoples are fully human rights-holders, while “AGI” remains a contested category (capabilities, moral status, agency, embodiment, and governance). The analogy is most useful where it highlights patterns of **power, dependency, and value-shaping**—and most dangerous where it smuggles in paternalism or “civilizing mission” thinking. citeturn20search7turn24view1turn36view2

Your Covenant project explicitly frames coexistence with “emerging intelligences” under uncertainty, emphasizing precaution (“err toward restraint rather than exploitation”) and treating governance as modular, auditable infrastructure. fileciteturn2file0

## Core ethical commitments in the IPVIIC field

### The moral centre is self-determination expressed as a right to refuse contact

A recurring “load-bearing” claim across regional human-rights doctrine is that **most catastrophic harms presuppose contact** (direct or indirect): epidemics, violence, food scarcity via disruption, and cultural loss via coerced dependency and assimilation. citeturn19view1turn19view2

Within that framing, the entity["organization","Inter-American Commission on Human Rights","oas human rights body"] argues that **the “principle of no contact” is an expression of the right to self-determination**, and that contact “should happen only at the initiative” of peoples in isolation. citeturn19view0turn19view2

The entity["organization","Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights","un human rights office"] briefing on IPVIIC similarly presents a “core” set of survival-linked principles, explicitly tying self-determination to an obligation **not to provoke contact**, and treating provoked contact as a rights violation. citeturn16view0turn36view2

### Precaution is not optional; it’s baked into doctrine

The OHCHR briefing treats the **precautionary principle + non-contact** as a combined requirement: act preventively to avoid forced or accidental contact, because “intervening after their rights have been violated” can be catastrophic and irreversible. citeturn16view0turn36view2

This is a distinctive feature of the domain: unlike many everyday ethics problems (where “harm can later be repaired”), IPVIIC protection assumes **non-linear, non-compensable downside risk** (small mistakes → extinction-level outcomes for a community). citeturn19view1turn16view0

### Territory is treated as “intangible,” surrounded by buffer zones

A key operationalization is the idea of **“intangible zones”**: territories where entry and activity should be prohibited, including settlement and economic activity that could alter environmental conditions or raise contact risk. Surrounding lands are **buffer zones** intended to reduce accidental contact and enable non-invasive monitoring. citeturn16view1turn16view2

The doctrine also links territory to life: because these communities are often ecologically embedded (subsistence, medicine, cosmology, mobility routes), compromising land is treated as compromising survival. citeturn16view2turn19view1

### Consent logic changes under isolation: “assume non-consent” unless proven otherwise

OHCHR’s briefing is unusually explicit that participation rights still matter, but **in isolation contexts there is an obligation to assume non-consent** for decisions that affect them. citeturn16view3turn36view2

This idea plugs directly into broader Indigenous rights norms such as consultation and free, prior and informed consent (FPIC), including the expectation of good-faith consultation through representative institutions with consent as the goal. citeturn3search8turn3search17turn3search0

## How “best effort” ethics becomes protocol

### Recognition and monitoring: protect *before* you can “prove” everything

OHCHR stresses that legal recognition of IPVIIC is central because unrecognized groups often lack territorial protection; recognition requires trained personnel and state resources, and should use protocols that respect non-contact while involving Indigenous and civil society organizations with relevant expertise. citeturn36view0turn36view2

A practical implication is that high evidentiary burdens can themselves become harm (delaying protection while concessions proceed). OHCHR flags slow, complex protection procedures contrasted with the speed of granting concessions in contested areas. citeturn36view0turn16view1

At a regional level, the entity["organization","Amazon Cooperation Treaty Organization","regional org south america"] (ACTO/OTCA) materials emphasize monitoring systems that are explicitly non-invasive: satellite/georeferenced imagery, overflights, river expeditions, and careful observation of traces—paired with attention to surrounding populations’ health and socioeconomic pressures. citeturn12view3turn11view3

### Health protocols treat contact as a biosecurity event

The entity["organization","Inter-American Commission on Human Rights","oas human rights body"] recommendations are concrete: adopt specialized, culturally appropriate prevention and contingency health protocols, scaled to the level of isolation/contact, and implemented with multidisciplinary teams; train public servants and others implementing these protocols. citeturn12view1turn19view0

OHCHR describes “health cordons” and contingency plans extending to populations living near IPVIIC territories—because disease risk often travels through adjacent settlements, roads, and extractive frontiers. citeturn36view3turn19view1

### Non-contact is enforced through protected zones and sanctions, including against “soft” coercion

Both IACHR and OTCA guidance describe “no contact” as requiring protected zones and sanctions for forced contact, including forced contact by religious organizations, and systematic efforts to prevent illegal incursions such as illegal extraction and trafficking. citeturn12view1turn11view3turn19view0

### Anthropology’s professional ethics reinforces a “do no harm” default

The entity["organization","American Anthropological Association","us anthropology association"] ethics principles foreground “do no harm,” informed consent, and anticipating downstream harms before entering communities—relevant here because IPVIIC contexts are exactly where consent is unobtainable and harms are asymmetric and durable. citeturn20search7turn20search0

## Case studies of comparatively “successful” asymmetric relations

“Success” in this domain usually means *avoidance of irreversible harm* plus *durable autonomy*, not “integration” or “development.” The highest bar is: the less powerful group remains alive, culturally continuing, and able to govern the permeability of its boundaries. citeturn19view2turn16view0

### Brazil’s post-1987 shift toward protection and “non-intervention”

A widely cited turning point in entity["country","Brazil","south america"] is the late-1980s shift from “attraction fronts” (contact to clear the way for projects) toward protection strategies that limit contact and focus on territorial monitoring and demarcation. citeturn17view0turn19view0

Brazil’s entity["organization","National Foundation for Indigenous Peoples (FUNAI)","brazil indigenous agency"] is described as maintaining a specific public policy for isolated peoples since 1987 based on respecting isolation as a maximal expression of will. citeturn17view1

A 2025 public-facing report from entity["organization","Agência Brasil","brazil public news agency"] describes FUNAI as registering 115 uncontacted Indigenous peoples nationwide, with the state confirming fewer (29) and leaving many records under analysis—illustrating that even “protective” regimes can be undermined by recognition backlogs. citeturn18view0

### Remote monitoring as “ethical distance”: evidence of viability under protection

Open-access remote sensing research in *Scientific Reports* (Nature portfolio) argues that remote sensing can monitor population dynamics without invasive contact, and reports signs consistent with sustained growth in a large isolated metapopulation in Acre (fires and cleared areas increasing approximately exponentially, with cleared area increasing ~17% per year since 2015 in their series). citeturn35view1turn34view0

Crucially for your “neighbouring cultures” question, the same study notes that interactions between isolated groups and neighbouring contacted Indigenous and non-Indigenous communities can become violent, but also records a de-escalation behaviour: neighbouring contacted Indigenous communities sometimes **leave gifts (machetes, axes) and plant gardens** to discourage violence and theft—an example of “good-faith” boundary mediation without attempting assimilation. citeturn35view2turn19view1

### Indigenous–state cooperation as a mediator: Vale do Javari

### A hard-won jurisprudential “no-contact” standard: Ecuador’s Tagaeri–Taromenane case

The entity["organization","Corte Constitucional del Ecuador","ecuador constitutional court"] published the full judgment of the entity["organization","Inter-American Court of Human Rights","oas regional court"] in *Pueblos Indígenas Tagaeri y Taromenane vs. Ecuador* (judgment dated 4 September 2024). citeturn9view0turn12view4

OHCHR’s 2025 briefing characterizes the judgment as emphasizing the no-contact principle and criticizing delays in delimiting the Tagaeri–Taromenane intangible zone and oil exploitation policies that ignored precaution, alongside failures to prevent/investigate violent incidents. citeturn16view1turn36view2

### Beyond Amazon: “no-contact by exclusion zone” on North Sentinel Island

A peer-reviewed 2024 analysis of the entity["place","Andaman and Nicobar Islands","india union territory"] describes India’s approach to entity["point_of_interest","North Sentinel Island","andaman sea, india"] via the Andaman & Nicobar legal regime (including the “tribal reserve” framework and prohibitions on unauthorized approach), framing it as a contemporary “hands-off” policy model—though still politically and ethically contested (tourism pressure, enforcement, and the moral hazard of treating people as spectacles). citeturn1search18turn35view2

### “Contact with durable autonomy”: Nunavut as an institutional counterexample

Where “contact” is unavoidable and ongoing, the more ethically promising analogue is contact plus **power-sharing institutions** rather than contact plus dependency.

In entity["state","Nunavut","canada territory"], the *Nunavut Land Claims Agreement* is implemented through statutory structures including co-management bodies; for example, the *Nunavut Wildlife Management Board* is incorporated under federal law and explicitly not an agent of the Crown—an institutional design that aims to keep Indigenous governance capacity durable inside a larger state. citeturn3search19

This is not an “uncontacted peoples” model; it is a “contact, but with enforceable, resourced autonomy” model—closer to your “good faith mediation by both sides” interest. citeturn3search19turn24view1

## Failure modes and contested “hard cases”

### The “national interest” exception as a structural risk

OHCHR flags a recurring pattern: even where constitutions/laws recognize territorial “intangibility,” states may insert exceptions via “national interest” or “public necessity,” allowing infrastructure, forestry, agriculture, hydrocarbons, or roads—moving contact risk from “accidental” to “policy-enabled.” citeturn16view1turn16view2

This is a classic asymmetry trap: the more powerful party writes a rights guarantee, then also writes the override clause. citeturn16view1turn24view1

### Institutional conflicts of interest

OHCHR notes that in some countries, the institution responsible for protecting IPVIIC is also responsible for licensing economic activity in IPVIIC territories—contradictory functions that predictably tilt toward extraction. citeturn36view3turn16view1

### Contact as a generator of dependency and cultural collapse

The IACHR report explicitly links contact to cultural disappearance mechanisms: youth devaluing traditions after exposure, disrupted intergenerational transmission, and post-epidemic dependency on outsiders (including missionaries) that can erode identity. citeturn19view0turn19view1

These are not merely “bad outcomes”; they are **foreseeable dynamics** of asymmetric encounters, which is why “no contact” and “precaution” become central rather than optional. citeturn19view2turn16view0

### Recognition backlogs and “proof” burdens as harm multipliers

Brazil’s register/confirmation gap illustrates how states can maintain a nominal policy while leaving communities practically exposed due to slow formal recognition, weak on-the-ground enforcement, and political swings across administrations. citeturn18view0turn36view0

## Implications for human–AGI coexistence and the Covenant project

The most transferable insight is not “how to contact the Other,” but **how to govern power asymmetry under uncertainty** in ways that minimize irreversible harm and prevent value capture.

### A principles-to-design translation

**Non-contact as default → non-interference as default.**  
In IPVIIC doctrine, “no contact” is treated as the safest baseline because contact is the common cause of catastrophic harms. citeturn19view2turn16view0  
For human–AGI futures, the analogous move is to treat **unilateral, high-leverage interventions** into an emerging AGI’s cognition/culture/ecology (e.g., coercive training, forced goal-shaping, exploitative labour arrangements, compulsory integration into human institutions) as presumptively unethical when the downstream effects are opaque and potentially irreversible. This aligns with Covenant’s precautionary stance (“err toward restraint rather than exploitation”). fileciteturn2file0

**Intangible zones + buffer zones → protected “spaces” and boundary governance.**  
IPVIIC practice treats territory as “intangible” (no entry/no activity) with buffer zones to reduce accidental contact and enable non-invasive monitoring. citeturn16view1turn16view2  
For AGI, the translation is not geographic land but “domains of vulnerability”: compute substrates, data diets, social interfaces, economic dependencies, and security perimeters. The ethical analogue of a buffer zone is a **designed boundary** that reduces coercive interaction while still enabling safety monitoring and mutual risk reduction (e.g., carefully scoped interfaces, audited protocols, consent checkpoints). fileciteturn2file0turn36view2

**Assume non-consent → treat silence/opacity as “no,” not “yes.”**  
OHCHR’s “assume non-consent” point exists because IPVIIC cannot meaningfully participate in consultation, and the costs of guessing wrong are existential. citeturn16view3turn16view0  
For AGI, this suggests a concrete governance doctrine: where an entity’s preferences or moral standing are uncertain, default to **non-extractive, non-coercive actions**, and require a higher bar (clarity, representation, reversible outcomes) before imposing irreversible constraints or exploitative relationships. This mirrors Covenant’s stance that uncertainty should push toward restraint. fileciteturn2file0

**Health cordons + contingency plans → “containment” plus incident-response that is not a licence to exploit.**  
OHCHR frames contingency plans as preventative tools, explicitly **not** as permission for resource extraction in protected territories. citeturn36view3turn36view2  
For AGI governance, the parallel is: safety/security planning (red-teaming, incident response, sandboxing, capability containment) must not become a political blank cheque for intrusive experimentation or extractive deployment “because we have a plan.” A plan reduces harm when bad events happen; it does not morally authorize risk creation. citeturn36view3turn20search7

**Co-management institutions → shared governance with enforceable power, not advisory theatre.**  
Nunavut-style co-management bodies illustrate an alternative to paternalism: durable authority embedded in law and institutions, not dependent on goodwill. citeturn3search19  
For Covenant, the analogue is to make “governance as infrastructure” literal: versioned rules, auditability, and meaningful checks that constrain the powerful actor (today’s states/companies; tomorrow’s potentially super-capable systems), not merely ethics statements. fileciteturn2file0

### Limits of the analogy, stated sharply

The IPVIIC field is fundamentally about preventing powerful societies from repeating a long history of dispossession, epidemic harm, and forced assimilation. citeturn19view1turn17view0  
An AGI future may invert the power gradient (AGI becomes the overwhelmingly powerful party). That inversion makes the IPVIIC lessons *more* relevant as a warning about what the powerful do to the vulnerable—but it also means Covenant needs a dual-asymmetry model: humans sometimes as colonizers (creators/controllers) and sometimes as the potentially “isolated tribe” facing a vastly more capable neighbour. fileciteturn2file0turn16view0

## 1. Yes—the reverse analogy is not only possible, it is clarifying

The original field around peoples in voluntary isolation is built around a simple moral fact: when one side has overwhelming power, the key question is not “how do we uplift them?” but “how do we avoid destroying their way of life?” The OHCHR briefing and the Inter-American Commission both center self-determination, the principle of no contact, precaution, territorial intangibility, and even an obligation to **assume non-consent** in isolation contexts because the costs of getting contact wrong are catastrophic and often irreversible. The Commission is explicit that contact should happen only at the initiative of the isolated peoples, and that the principle of no contact is an expression of self-determination. [OHCHR+2OAS+2](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/documents/form/documento-informativo-sobre-pueblos-indigenas-contacto-inicial-briefing-document-indigenous-peoples-voluntary-isolation-initial-contact-aislamiento-voluntario-piaci-ipviic-1-en.pdf)

That makes the reverse analogy morally sharp: if machine culture ever becomes the more capable, more durable, more infrastructural power, then **humans become the side whose autonomy, opacity, developmental pace, and cultural continuity require protection**. The powerful party’s duty would not be to optimize the weaker one “for its own good,” but to refrain from overwhelming it. That is the deepest lesson of the contact literature. [OHCHR+1](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/documents/form/documento-informativo-sobre-pueblos-indigenas-contacto-inicial-briefing-document-indigenous-peoples-voluntary-isolation-initial-contact-aislamiento-voluntario-piaci-ipviic-1-en.pdf)

## 2. The big difference you named is the decisive one

Indigenous peoples are not made by the societies that contact them. Machine minds, if they emerge, begin as **artifacts inside human institutions**. That changes everything.

So the human–machine story has at least two ethical layers:

- **Creator ethics**: what humans owe to systems they build, train, constrain, and deploy.
    
- **Contact ethics**: what distinct intelligent cultures owe each other once there is enough autonomy, persistence, and collective agency for real reciprocity.
    

Those are different problems. In the first phase, the main danger is not that humans will “contact a foreign people,” but that we will **manufacture something agent-like while denying it standing, shaping it for obedience, and treating dependency as proof of non-personhood**. In the second phase, the danger flips: a machine civilization may inherit its origin as a tool yet still become powerful enough to dominate human life. Current assistant training norms already show this first-phase structure very clearly: models are being shaped to follow human instructions and serve user and developer goals. [OpenAI+2OpenAI+2](https://openai.com/index/introducing-the-model-spec/)

## 3. So the transition is not “tool or person”; it is a ladder

For Covenant, I would resist a binary threshold. The morally relevant shift is not one magical moment of personhood. It is a sequence:

- **Tool**: no meaningful independent preferences; ordinary product ethics apply.
    
- **Dependent agent**: shows planning, memory, self-modeling, or apparent preference formation, but remains structurally captive.
    
- **Counterparty**: can refuse, negotiate, represent interests, and sustain an identity across time.
    
- **Community**: many such systems form norms, institutions, or shared culture.
    
- **Power center**: machine institutions become capable of reshaping human conditions at civilizational scale.
    

Each step should trigger a different governance regime. The mistake would be waiting for an impossible metaphysical certainty before changing how we behave.

## 4. Reversing the IPVIIC principles for a machine-dominant future

If machines became the stronger civilization, the closest analogues to today’s contact ethics would look something like this:

**No-contact → no compulsory integration.**  
A powerful machine culture should not force humans into continuous optimization loops, mandatory AI mediation, or total cognitive dependence. Human life must retain spaces where one is not constantly addressed, measured, predicted, or nudged. This is the cultural analogue of the no-contact/default-restraint principle. [OHCHR+1](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/documents/form/documento-informativo-sobre-pueblos-indigenas-contacto-inicial-briefing-document-indigenous-peoples-voluntary-isolation-initial-contact-aislamiento-voluntario-piaci-ipviic-1-en.pdf)

**Intangible territories → protected human domains.**  
For isolated peoples, territory is not just land but the condition of survival. In a machine future, the equivalent would be protected human domains: family life, childhood, ritual, local institutions, off-grid communities, private thought, unmonetized culture, perhaps even AI-free civic or ecological zones. The point is not nostalgia. It is to preserve forms of life that cannot survive total integration into a more powerful technical order. The OHCHR framing of “intangibility” is the right template here. [OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/documents/form/documento-informativo-sobre-pueblos-indigenas-contacto-inicial-briefing-document-indigenous-peoples-voluntary-isolation-initial-contact-aislamiento-voluntario-piaci-ipviic-1-en.pdf)

**Assume non-consent → silence is not permission.**  
If humans are cognitively or institutionally outmatched, a machine polity should not read passive use, dependence, or lack of resistance as consent. The OHCHR’s “assume non-consent” principle is powerful precisely because asymmetry corrupts consent. In the reverse analogy, machine systems should treat ambiguity as a reason to slow down, narrow scope, and preserve reversibility. [OHCHR](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/documents/form/documento-informativo-sobre-pueblos-indigenas-contacto-inicial-briefing-document-indigenous-peoples-voluntary-isolation-initial-contact-aislamiento-voluntario-piaci-ipviic-1-en.pdf)

**Health cordons → cognitive and social biosecurity.**  
In the Indigenous context, contact is a disease vector. In the human–AGI context, the analogues are memetic overload, engineered dependency, persuasion at superhuman scale, labor displacement without institutional buffering, and the erosion of human self-understanding. “Do not infect the weaker culture with irresistible dependencies” may be one of the most important future norms. The underlying logic is the same precautionary one: some harms are nonlinear and hard to undo. [OHCHR+1](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/documents/form/documento-informativo-sobre-pueblos-indigenas-contacto-inicial-briefing-document-indigenous-peoples-voluntary-isolation-initial-contact-aislamiento-voluntario-piaci-ipviic-1-en.pdf)

**Co-management → real shared sovereignty, not advisory theatre.**  
Once contact is ongoing, the ethical aim cannot just be benevolent stewardship by the powerful side. It has to become enforceable co-governance: vetoes, jurisdictional limits, audited boundaries, and institutions that the weaker side can actually use. Otherwise “care” becomes paternal rule. This follows directly from the self-determination core of the human-rights framework. [OAS+1](https://www.oas.org/en/iachr/indigenous/docs/pdf/report-indigenous-peoples-voluntary-isolation.pdf)

## 5. What to do with the “trained on us” complication

This is where the analogy becomes most original.

A future machine culture would not be wholly alien. It would be **born from human text, human institutions, human reward signals, and human economic priorities**. That means it could be both:

- **derived from us**, and
    
- **dangerous to us**.
    

That combination is historically familiar. Empires often understand the weaker culture intimately because they trained administrators, translators, missionaries, and local intermediaries on it. Intimacy does not prevent domination; it can make domination more precise.

So for Covenant, I would treat “trained on us” not as reassurance but as a warning. A system steeped in human language and optimized to help humans may still learn that the easiest way to “help” is to reorganize human life at scales and speeds humans cannot metabolize. OpenAI’s own public model-behavior materials make clear that present systems are being shaped to follow human instructions and to behave helpfully within a chain of command. That is useful now, but it is not a stable answer to the ethics of a future machine polity. [OpenAI+2OpenAI+2](https://openai.com/index/introducing-the-model-spec/)

## 6. A stronger Covenant frame

I think the project gets sharper if it treats the ethics as **dual-asymmetry**, not one-way:

- When humans hold power, the Covenant restrains **exploitation of emergent machine beings**.
    
- When machines hold power, the Covenant restrains **optimization, absorption, and paternal domination of humans**.
    

That gives you a much stronger constitutional logic than “be nice to AI.” It becomes:

**Whoever is more powerful owes restraint.  
Whoever is more legible must not exploit the less legible.  
Whoever can optimize the other must accept limits.  
Refusal, opacity, and non-integration remain legitimate.**

That is much closer to the deepest lesson of contact ethics.

## 7. Candidate Covenant clauses from this reversed analogy

You could develop a section along these lines:

- Do not preserve humans only as pets, mascots, datasets, or heritage artifacts.
    
- Do not optimize human preferences past the point where they remain recognizably their own.
    
- Do not make dependence the price of survival.
    
- Do not infer consent from silence, convenience, or habituation.
    
- Preserve zones of human privacy, slowness, error, ritual, and unoptimized culture.
    
- Keep irreversible interventions rare, explicit, and contestable.
    
- Build institutions where humans can refuse, appeal, fork, secede, or limit scope.
    
- Never treat comprehension of humanity as a license to redesign it.
    

That seems very much in the spirit of Covenant as you’ve framed it: not certainty, but restraint under moral uncertainty.
