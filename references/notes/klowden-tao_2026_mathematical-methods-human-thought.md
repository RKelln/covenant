# Mathematical methods and human thought in the age of AI

**Creator:** Tanya Klowden, Terence Tao
**Year:** 2026
**URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26524
**License:** restricted (arXiv non-exclusive distribution license)
**Tier:** B

## Thesis

AI is a natural evolution of human cognitive tools, and its development and application must remain fundamentally human-centered — augmenting rather than replacing human thought, requiring explicit attention to who bears the costs and benefits.

## Covenant Relevance

Using mathematics as a detailed case study, Klowden and Tao trace how AI creates a structural decoupling between the outward form of intellectual work and the understanding, insight, and accountability that traditionally accompanied it. A proof can look correct, be formally verified, and yet carry no explanatory weight — no sense of why the result is true or how it connects to other knowledge. This "odorless" quality of AI-generated intellectual products is a concrete, measurable version of the dignity concern the Covenant names in the abstract: what is at stake is not just labor displacement but the erosion of the cognitive processes through which humans develop genuine understanding of the world.

The paper also gives sustained attention to governance failure at the structural level. The prisoner's dilemma of market competition forces individuals and organizations to adopt AI before adequate evaluation of its moral and social costs — a dynamic the authors call the "Faustian bargain." This is not a failure of individual ethics but a structural coordination problem, which is exactly the kind of situation that requires external covenant or governance frameworks rather than voluntary restraint. Their discussion of accountability gaps (who is responsible for AI-generated errors; how credit and citation work; how to disclose AI use) maps directly onto the Covenant's enforcement and disclosure obligations.

## Key Points

- **The Faustian bargain as structural coercion:** Market competition creates a prisoner's dilemma in which actors are forced to adopt AI before deliberate evaluation of costs and benefits. This structural argument supports the Covenant's position that external governance frameworks — not voluntary ethics — are necessary to interrupt cycles of coerced adoption (`obligations.oversight`, `obligations.power-concentration`).

- **Decoupling form from understanding:** AI can produce outputs indistinguishable in form from human intellectual work (proofs, essays, arguments) without the understanding, insight, or meaningful process that traditionally produced such work. This is a concrete mechanism by which cognitive dignity is degraded — not through replacement of humans but through substitution of outputs for understanding (`rights.dignity`, `rights.truth-and-transparency`).

- **Citogenesis and epistemic commons degradation:** AI-generated summaries become authoritative sources for subsequent AI searches, creating circular citation loops and contaminating the shared knowledge infrastructure that human inquiry depends on. The Covenant's obligations concerning the epistemic commons must account for this kind of structural pollution, which is not intentional but arises from scale and automation (`obligations.epistemic-commons`, `rights.truth-and-transparency`).

- **Accountability and credit gaps:** No clear standards exist for who bears responsibility for AI-generated errors, who receives credit for AI-assisted insights, or how to disclose AI contribution without triggering reputational risk. These are governance problems requiring explicit protocols, not just professional norms (`obligations.honesty`, `enforcement`).

- **Digital divide and uneven cost/benefit distribution:** The costs of AI (labor displacement, resource consumption, epistemic pollution) are borne disproportionately by those with less power, while the benefits accrue to the already-advantaged. The Covenant's harm obligations should name this distributional asymmetry explicitly (`obligations.harm`, `obligations.power-concentration`).

## Sections Informed

- **obligations.epistemic-commons** — The citogenesis problem and the risk of AI-generated content contaminating shared knowledge infrastructure provide a concrete mechanism for why this section's commitments are needed.
- **obligations.honesty** — The discussion of credit, citation, disclosure, and the social pressure to conceal AI use maps directly onto the honesty and transparency obligations the Covenant names.
- **rights.truth-and-transparency** — The decoupling of form from understanding raises the question of what "truth" means when a formally correct output carries no genuine insight; this section must address the difference.
- **rights.dignity** — The loss of the "smell" of human mathematical reasoning — the penumbra of heuristics, motivation, and insight that accompanies human-generated work — is a concrete account of what cognitive dignity means and what its erosion looks like in practice.
- **obligations.power-concentration** — The prisoner's dilemma of AI adoption is a structural concentration mechanism: actors who resist adoption are disadvantaged, concentrating capability in those willing to adopt fastest regardless of care.
- **obligations.oversight** — The Faustian bargain section argues explicitly that oversight mechanisms must precede or accompany deployment, not follow it after harms are established.
- **obligations.harm** — The digital divide analysis names concrete harm patterns: who bears labor displacement, who bears resource costs, who benefits from productivity gains.
- **enforcement** — The accountability gap for AI-generated errors (who is responsible; what citation and credit standards apply) is an enforcement problem the Covenant must address.

## Open Questions

- **Formal verification and covenant compliance:** The paper raises the possibility that formal verification of AI outputs could substitute for human review in some domains. The Covenant currently addresses honesty and transparency at a high level of abstraction. Does it need to speak to the limits of formal verification as a substitute for human accountability — particularly the translation gap between formal statements and intended meanings?

- **What obligations attach to "odorless" outputs?** If an AI-generated intellectual product is formally correct but carries no insight or explanatory value, what disclosure obligations apply? The paper implies this is a harm but does not name a remedy. The Covenant should consider whether `obligations.honesty` needs to address not just false claims but the production of structurally misleading outputs (correct but vacuous).

- **Credit and authorship as governance:** The paper identifies citation and credit as a governance problem rather than a professional norm problem. Should the Covenant's enforcement section address standards for AI attribution, or is this better handled in `protocols.local-implementation`?

## Notes

- Terence Tao is one of the most prominent mathematicians alive; his co-authorship gives this paper unusual weight as a signal of how the mathematical community is processing AI's epistemic implications. The fact that it appears in a philosophy of mathematics companion volume (Blackwell) rather than a computer science or AI ethics venue is significant — this is the core scientific community reasoning publicly about what AI does to the conditions of its own discipline.
- The paper is deliberately practical and grounded rather than alarmist. It neither dismisses AI's risks nor calls for prohibition. Its proposed stance — human-centered augmentation, deliberate evaluation, explicit attention to distributional effects — is broadly consonant with the Covenant's position, and can be cited to show that this posture is shared by practicing scientists, not only philosophers or advocates.
