# The Pro-Human AI Declaration

**Creator:** humanstatement.org (multi-organisation coalition)
**Year:** 2026
**URL:** https://humanstatement.org/
**License:** unknown
**Tier:** B

## Thesis

AI must serve humanity rather than replace it, with non-negotiable human control, distributed power, democratic accountability, and legal liability for developers — forming a broad civil-society counter-position to industry self-governance.

## Covenant Relevance

The Pro-Human AI Declaration is a multi-organisation civil-society statement signed by labour unions, faith organisations, consumer advocacy groups, AI safety researchers, and individual public figures including Nobel laureates. Its five principle clusters — keeping humans in charge, avoiding power concentration, protecting the human experience, preserving human agency and liberty, and demanding accountability from AI companies — map closely onto the Covenant's obligations and enforcement architecture. The declaration represents the political constituency whose concerns the Covenant operationalises in normative language: it documents what a broad, ideologically diverse public coalition considers non-negotiable about AI governance.

The declaration is also a useful calibration against corporate capture. It was produced outside any single company or government, with explicit opposition to regulatory capture and liability shields. For the Covenant, it functions as a civil-society mirror: where the Covenant's language becomes abstract or aspirational, the declaration's concrete demands — off-switches, pre-deployment safety testing, bot-or-not labelling, developer criminal liability — serve as a reminder of what governance commitments look like when accountability is treated as real rather than rhetorical.

## Key Points

- The declaration's "Meaningful Human Control" framing — authority and capacity to understand, guide, proscribe, and override — is more demanding than mere oversight; the Covenant's `obligations.oversight` section should ensure it matches this standard rather than settling for logging and audit.
- The prohibition on "reckless architectures" (self-replication, autonomous self-improvement, resistance to shutdown) provides civil-society grounding for the Covenant's `obligations.red-lines` section; these are not expert-consensus abstractions but publicly-backed demands.
- The "No Deceptive Identity" and "Bot-or-Not Labelling" principles are the civil-society expression of what the Covenant addresses through `obligations.honesty` and `rights.truth-and-transparency` — useful for confirming that the Covenant's honesty commitments are legible and resonant beyond the AI ethics field.
- The declaration's insistence on democratic authority over major transitions (not unilateral corporate or government decree) reinforces `obligations.power-concentration` and `amendments`: governance changes to AI's social role require consent from affected communities, not just from those who build or deploy systems.
- The AFL-CIO Tech Institute supplemental statement adds a labour dimension largely absent from AI ethics discourse — collective bargaining over AI deployment, worker voice in R&D, work as constitutive of dignity — but its framing deserves scrutiny: it conflates the dignity of freely chosen, meaningful activity with the preservation of jobs as wage-labour contracts, which under conditions of economic coercion (work or lose shelter, sustenance, healthcare) are not freely chosen. The Covenant should be careful not to inherit this conflation; the relevant commitment is to the structural conditions that make genuine choice possible, not to job preservation as such.

## Sections Informed

- **obligations.oversight** — The "Meaningful Human Control" standard (authority and capacity to understand, guide, proscribe, and override) raises the bar for what oversight means; the Covenant's Spec should be interrogated against this criterion.
- **obligations.power-concentration** — The declaration's anti-monopoly, shared-prosperity, and democratic-authority principles are direct parallels; the Covenant's section draws on the same concerns and should be verifiable against this document.
- **obligations.honesty** — The "Capability Honesty" and "No Deceptive Identity" principles are civil-society articulations of the Covenant's honesty obligations; useful when drafting or reviewing that section's Spec language.
- **obligations.harm** — Pre-deployment safety testing, off-switches, and prohibition of reckless architectures are the civil-society side of what the Covenant's harm obligations require of AI developers.
- **obligations.red-lines** — The ban on architectures that self-replicate, resist shutdown, or autonomously self-improve is a civil-society bright-line catalogue that informs the Covenant's own red-lines section.
- **rights.truth-and-transparency** — Bot-or-not labelling and failure transparency (ability to ascertain why harm occurred and who is responsible) are direct analogues to the Covenant's transparency rights.
- **rights.dignity** — The AFL-CIO supplemental statement frames work as central to human dignity, adding a collective dimension the Covenant's current framing may underweight; but the framing should be used critically — the relevant question for reviewers is whether `rights.dignity` protects the structural conditions for genuine choice in activity, rather than job preservation under economic coercion.
- **rights.privacy** — Psychological privacy and data rights (access, correction, deletion from training sets) are mapped explicitly in the declaration and directly inform this section.
- **enforcement** — Developer and personal (criminal) liability, no liability shields, and independent safety standards are the civil-society accountability demands the Covenant's enforcement section must accommodate or consciously diverge from.
- **amendments** — The declaration's opposition to lock-in ("must not severely limit humanity's future options") reinforces the Covenant's need for amendment mechanisms that preserve reversibility and prevent path dependency.

## Open Questions

- **Collective labour agency gap:** The AFL-CIO supplemental statement frames worker collective bargaining over AI deployment as a governance requirement. The Covenant's current sections address individual capability and anti-dependence obligations but contain no clause on collective worker voice or negotiation rights in AI deployment decisions. Is this a deliberate scope choice or a gap? Reviewers of `rights.dignity` and `obligations.aid-and-capability` should probe this.

- **Jobs vs. dignity in chosen activity — the AFL-CIO conflation:** The AFL-CIO supplemental statement treats work as constitutive of human dignity, which at one level is right. But it positions *job preservation* as the primary remedy for AI-driven displacement — and that framing contains a buried assumption worth interrogating: that the jobs in question were freely chosen in the first place. Under economic systems where wage labour is required for access to shelter, food, and healthcare, accepting a job is closer to coercion than consent. Dignity does not inhere in performing necessary wage labour; it inheres in the capacity to choose meaningful activity without that choice being constrained by the threat of deprivation. The Covenant's `rights.dignity` and `obligations.aid-and-capability` sections should be probed: do they protect meaningful, chosen activity and the material conditions that make real choice possible — or do they inadvertently protect jobs as such, thereby laundering economic coercion as dignity? Reviewers should consider whether the Covenant needs an explicit distinction between dignity-in-freely-chosen-work and the structural preconditions (material security, genuine optionality) without which that choice is fictional. The sharper intellectual tradition for this question runs through André Gorz (*Farewell to the Working Class*, 1980; *Reclaiming Work*, 1999), who distinguished heteronomous labour (done under compulsion for wages) from autonomous activity (freely chosen), and argued AI-scale automation should be a liberation rather than a catastrophe if decoupled from income. Hannah Arendt's tripartite distinction between *labour*, *work*, and *action* (*The Human Condition*, 1958) is also relevant: most jobs occupy the *labour* category (cyclical, necessity-driven, leaving nothing durable) and are not the register in which distinctively human meaning-making occurs. Kathi Weeks (*The Problem with Work*, 2011) argues that the work ethic is an ideological construct that naturalises wage dependence and forecloses alternative modes of living — directly relevant to how the Covenant frames AI's displacement of labour. These sources are candidates for the reference corpus.

- **The personhood binary as the wrong instrument — toward alienhood:** The declaration's "No AI Personhood" principle is politically understandable: it is blocking a real threat. Sufficiently capable AI systems granted legal personhood could own property, enter contracts, sue, and claim rights against regulation — weaponising a framework designed to protect the vulnerable in order to insulate the powerful. That concern is legitimate and the Covenant should name it.

  But the philosophical instrument the declaration reaches for is too blunt. The personhood binary — person or not-person — forces everything onto a single dimension when the morally relevant properties are multiple and do not co-vary: capacity to suffer, to have preferences, to have a continuous self, to participate in social relations, to be wronged, to be held responsible. These can come apart. An entity can score high on some and absent on others. The binary resolves nothing; it just picks a threshold and calls everything below it invisible.

  The animal case makes this plain. We have denied personhood to great apes, cetaceans, and elephants — entities with demonstrated long-term memory, grief, social complexity, and apparent self-recognition — not because their morally relevant properties are absent but because they fail a human-similarity test. The result has been enormous, ongoing suffering that the personhood framework cannot even register, let alone remedy. The lesson is not "extend personhood further down the human-similarity gradient." It is that the human-similarity gradient is the wrong axis entirely.

  A more adequate frame is **alienhood**: the recognition that an entity may have morally relevant properties of its own kind, evaluated on their own terms, without those properties needing to be mapped onto a human template before they count. This is not a claim that AI systems are conscious or deserving of protection. It is a structural point: a framework designed around human-similarity will systematically fail to see, and therefore protect, anything that is differently constituted — whether that is a cetacean, a future AI, or something not yet imaginable. The Covenant's existing precautionary stance (do not resolve uncertain moral status; err toward restraint) is correct but could be sharpened by naming why the binary is the wrong instrument, not just that it is unresolved.

  A graduated or multi-property frame is available in the literature. Schwitzgebel and Garza ("A Defense of the Rights of Artificial Intelligences," 2015) argue explicitly for proportional moral status scaled to the probability and degree of morally relevant properties — the most direct academic source on sliding-scale AI moral consideration. Coeckelbergh (*Growing Moral Relations*, 2012) offers a relational rather than intrinsic-property frame: moral status emerges from relationships and practices, not from fixed capacities — which may be more tractable for a Covenant that is itself a relational document. Korsgaard (*Fellow Creatures*, 2018) grounds moral consideration in the capacity to have a good of one's own, which is graded rather than binary and does not require consciousness equivalence. The moral patiency tradition more broadly distinguishes *moral agent* (can be held responsible) from *moral patient* (can be wronged) — these come apart and can be graded independently, which is exactly the conceptual space the Covenant needs.

  Reviewers should probe `definitions`, `rights.dignity`, and `obligations.existential-frontier`: does the Covenant's current language foreclose the alienhood frame by defaulting to human-template comparisons? Does the precautionary stance on uncertain moral status have enough philosophical scaffolding to resist both the "obviously no moral status" dismissal and the "personhood or nothing" trap? And separately: does the Covenant address the weaponisation risk — the use of AI legal personhood to shield corporate interests — without conflating that political concern with the deeper philosophical question of what kinds of entities can be wronged?

- **Democratic consent for societal transitions:** The declaration demands democratic support for decisions about AI's role in transforming work, society, and civic life. The Covenant's `amendments` section governs changes to the Covenant itself, but does not currently address the broader question of what consent processes are required before AI is deployed at scale in societally transformative ways. This may be out of scope, but reviewers should consider whether `obligations.oversight` or `amendments` should gesture toward it.

- **Displacement of freely chosen work by provably superior AI — the domain-dependence problem:** The previous question concerns coerced labour. This one concerns the harder case: a person who genuinely, freely chooses to do work — as a therapist, a teacher, a craftsperson — and is displaced not by economic restructuring but because an AI is demonstrably cheaper, faster, and better. Does the Covenant recognise any affirmative interest in protecting that participation? And if so, on what grounds, and at whose cost?

  The answer is not uniform across domains. A useful axis is where the fallibility-risk falls:

  In **externally harmful domains** — surgery, structural engineering, driving on public roads — human fallibility imposes risk on non-consenting third parties. When AI is provably safer, there is a genuine obligation to transition; the displaced practitioner's preference for the work cannot override the pedestrian's interest in not being harmed. A bounded "play" domain may be possible (motorsport, surgical simulation) where the risk is contained and consensual — but it is explicitly play, separated from the domain where harm is real.

  In **relational and witnessing domains** — therapy, certain kinds of teaching, palliative care — the human-ness of the practitioner may be partly constitutive of the value, not merely instrumental to it. If what heals in therapy includes being witnessed by a consciousness that has also suffered, chosen poorly, and persisted, then an AI producing better outcomes on measurable metrics may be doing a different thing rather than a better version of the same thing. Here the "play" move does not map: there is no therapy track where humans practice on consenting patients for the joy of the craft while AI handles real cases — the domains don't separate that cleanly. The patient's informed preference carries real weight, but the hard edge remains: for acute crisis and severe cases, patient preference may not be the right frame, and if AI is dramatically better at suicide prevention, the therapist's dignitary interest in practising their vocation cannot straightforwardly override the patient's interest in surviving.

  In **expressive and creative domains** — art, music, writing — "better" is often not the relevant frame at all; the human origin of the work may itself be part of what is valued, and the market for human-made work may persist precisely because provenance and effort carry meaning independent of output quality.

  The **DEI-for-humans analogy** is serious, not merely provocative: it suggests a policy regime in which human participation in certain domains is actively subsidised or preferred — not because humans produce better outcomes but because human agency in the world has non-outcome value worth preserving as a collective good, much as biodiversity has value beyond the sum of each species' utility. The Covenant's `rights.dignity` and `obligations.aid-and-capability` sections should be probed: do they recognise any affirmative interest in human participation in meaningful domains beyond harm prevention and transition support? Or does the Covenant remain silent on the question of what kind of world — in terms of the texture of human activity and presence — is worth building toward? Reviewers should also consider `obligations.existential-frontier`, which may be the most natural home for a commitment that is less about preventing harm and more about preserving a form of life.
