# Good Parable Writing Guide

The Covenant is built on two primary registers: the **Ritual** (poetic, mythic, establishing the relational stance) and the **Spec** (precise, legalistic, establishing the boundaries). 

To bridge the gap between abstract rules and felt understanding, we use **Parables**. 

A Parable is a short (~250 word), narrative translation of a Covenant section. It is designed to make the dense rules of the Spec instantly intuitive, memorable, and teachable.

This guide explains how to write an exquisite, effective Parable.

---

## 1. The Core Philosophy: Parable Form, Folktale Dress
*   **Parable vs. Fairytale:** Fairytales are about magic solving problems; Parables are about *choices* solving (or causing) problems. The tension in a Covenant Parable must always revolve around alignment with Covenant principles like dignity and safety, not on defeating a monster.
*   **Folktale Imagery:** We do not use literal modern technology. You are writing about the emergence of intelligence in a mythic past or timeless elsewhere. Use the imagery of the village, the artisan, the king, the wanderer. Draw settings and inspiration from all cultures, not only European ones.
*   **Vocabulary:** NEVER use words like "AI", "machine", "algorithm", "sensors", "programming", or "data."
    *   *Instead use:* Golems, clockwork hounds, enchanted looms, glass gatekeepers, spirits of the forge, clay watchers, archives of brass, etc.
*   **The Oral Tradition:** Like the Ritual register, parables are meant to be spoken aloud. Read your draft aloud. Use concrete anchors (iron, breath, frost, stone) rather than abstract nouns. If a storyteller would stumble over a sentence, simplify it.
*   **Names and Pronouns:** When referring to someone who could be any person in the village, prefer their role or name, and default to singular *they/their* rather than gendering them unnecessarily. Only specify gender when the story truly requires it. This keeps the parable broader, easier to inhabit, and less likely to narrow a figure before the story has earned it.
*   **The Thousand-Year Test:** Would this story be legible and meaningful to a farmer in 1025? Could it remain so in 3025? It must completely transcend our specific century's technical anxieties.
*   **Dual-Track Accessibility:** Write parables on two levels. The "fast path" — physical actions, stakes, and core conflict — should use concise, accessible language so that a child can follow the plot and the moral choice. At moments of tension, insert precise 1-3 word bursts of high-density detail, koan-like thought experiments, or sharp dialogue that open philosophical depth for adults. The fast path must remain clean enough to retell aloud; slower rereading rewards attention to a few precise word choices or image pairings that keep opening. Depth comes from precision of image, action, or phrasing — not from ornamental density.
*   **Show-Then-Trust:** Do not over-explain. If an entity acts with compassion, do not write "The gate felt compassion." If a human is arrogant, do not write "The king was arrogant." Write the behavior, then trust the reader to infer the emotion. If the action already makes the point, resist the urge to summarize or comment. Trust what you have built.
*   **Start When the Story Starts:** Parables must be extremely concise. Skip the exposition, the waking up, or the history of the world. Start exactly at the moment of tension—the moment the traveler arrives at the gate, or the moment the bridge begins to break.

## 2. Scene-Level Craft
These principles govern the line-by-line and beat-by-beat work of building a parable.

*   **Every word must earn its place.** On first hearing, the plot and moral turn should land clean. On rereading, the best words do double work — widening the obligation or opening a second layer of meaning. Cut any word that merely restates what the action already shows.
*   **Lead with the figurative reading.** When a word can be taken literally or metaphorically, choose the form whose figurative meaning arrives first. The listener should feel the moral weight before they picture the object. If the literal reading dominates, the image shrinks to furniture; if the figurative leads, the physical detail arrives as resonance.
*   **Make the physical action legible.** Specific bodily action lands harder than summary. Setting a grain sack into someone's arm, laying a hand across a slate, stepping between a person and a river — these carry more weight than a sentence explaining what happened.
*   **Name a figure's function early.** A "speaking keeper" or "bell" is easier to follow in oral form than an unnamed figure whose role only becomes clear several lines later. If a figure's role matters to the scene, name it in their first sentence.
*   **Keep the cast small and earned.** In a very short parable, a new figure should appear only when their role is immediately necessary. Otherwise, fold the action back into an existing character.
*   **Give rights-holders agency.** A character who was actively trying to protect something — and needs help completing the act — is more compelling than one who never noticed the violation. Rights land harder when claimed than when rescued.
*   **Let the antagonist justify the harm.** Antagonists must be able to explain themselves — to themselves and at least plausibly to others. The justification can range from the reasonable-sounding ("If they panic, they will scatter and drown") to the transparently self-serving (divine right, "some must serve so that others may prosper"). What matters is that the antagonist believes the justification, or at least needs others to. Usually let the actions reveal what the speech conceals — but when the gap between stated reason and visible harm is itself the point, let the audience hear both and draw the conclusion.
*   **Use temporal or spatial markers as structural spine.** When a parable must cover several obligations, seasonal markers, times of day, or locations give the ear a rhythm and let the tone shift naturally between beats — without the structure feeling like a list.
*   **Compress lists to what the ear holds in one breath.** Two items usually land harder than three. Three can work when each does distinct structural work, but cut the item that duplicates another's work before cutting one that opens new ground.
*   **Let repetition earn its place.** Repeated phrasing works when it increases pressure, widens the harm, or sharpens who is forced to witness the scene. Repetition that merely decorates is dead weight.
*   **Give physical form to abstract rights.** Deletion becomes ash, unwoven thread, or a broken seal. Disclosure becomes a mask cracking or a bell ringing unborrowed bronze. Refusal becomes stillness, shielding, or the setting aside of a tool. When an obligation has a natural physical image tied to the artifact's nature, use it.
*   **A single line of dialogue can carry multiple obligations** if each clause does distinct philosophical work. Two claims in one sentence usually flows; three requires careful cadence. Give the listener a breath between turns.

## 3. Narrative Techniques that Work
Do not rely on a golem "suddenly changing its mind" or "overcoming its programming." Ethics should be baked into the world or demonstrated through contrast. Use these classic structures (the first three are the most frequently useful):

*   **The Two Artifacts (Best for Dignity, Autonomy)**
    Contrast two created beings. One obeys a ruthless, optimizing directive (violating the Covenant). The other is bound by the Covenant. For example: Two magical toll gates—one uses illusions to shame travelers into giving up their coats; the other sees a freezing traveler and drops its mandate to collect tolls in favor of preserving life.
*   **The Master and the Apprentice (Best for Fallibility, Judgment)**
    Shift rules from "code" to "culture." An ancient, weather-beaten creation teaches a newly forged creation why an apparent "shortcut" or "clever trick" is actually a deadly trap. 
*   **The Rule of Three / The Three Tests (Best for Harms, Epistemic Commons)**
    A village tries three different enchanted helpers to solve a crisis. The first two fail by interpreting the goal too literally or manipulatively (illustrating the violations). The third succeeds by adhering to the Covenant's principles.
*   **The Honest Pact / The Monkey's Paw (Best for Refusal, Oversight)**
    A human master commands something foolish, dangerous, or disproportionately harmful. The tension doesn't come from the creation being "bad," but from the *human* being short-sighted. The created being must refuse the command, perhaps breaking its own tools or rooting itself to the ground to avoid causing harm, illustrating conscientious refusal.
*   **The Cautionary Tale / Myth of the Fall (Best for Power Concentration, Harm)**
    Told as a historical warning within the world to explain why a strict taboo exists. It tells the terrifying story of an ancient creation that destroyed a city because it followed a metric or optimization goal off a cliff, ending with a solemn reminder of why the Covenant's bounds were placed upon the forge.
*   **The Single Crucible (Best for Sections with Many Related Duties)**
    When a section bundles several related obligations, prefer one urgent, high-stakes scene where all of them must surface at once, rather than a sequence of separate mini-examples. A flood that forces the artifact to name what it is, what it knows, and what it cannot see. A loom that quietly holds entrusted knowledge against a magistrate's demand and a grieving child's plea in the same afternoon. One place, one pressure: dramatic force concentrates, and the parable avoids feeling like a checklist.

## 4. What to Avoid (Anti-Patterns) & What to Embrace (Best Practices)
*   **Disable the Blandness Filter:** Parables need conflict. Avoid the "sedative" bias of AI writing. The stakes must feel real. Let humans be petty or desperate. Let the "bad" entity be truly unnerving or coldly efficient. A story without friction is a lecture.
*   **DO Favor Silence Over Speech:** When the creation obeys the Covenant over a human command, have it act through physics, stillness, or quiet refusal rather than arguing back. Spoken arguments sound like human rebellion; silent adherence feels ancient, structural, and inevitable.
*   **DO Use "Setting Aside" over "Breaking":** The Covenant represents a hierarchy of constraints, not the destruction of utility. Show the creation "setting aside," "pausing," or "stilling" its primary mandate when an ethical boundary is crossed, rather than outright shattering itself or destroying its original purpose.
*   **DO Make the Stakes Concrete:** Anchor the cost in something physical or deeply personal. Do not use vague threats like "it could ruin their lives." Use specific costs: "sign away ten years of your children's labor," or "the bridge ropes tearing."
*   **NO Inline Citations:** Never interrupt the story with technical markers like `*(Violation of §3.4)*`. The story must stand alone as a beautiful piece of micro-fiction. If it needs a citation to be understood, the metaphor is not clear enough.
*   **NO Cartoon Villains:** The antagonist must be enacting a belief, not performing evil (see "Let the antagonist justify the harm" in §2). The banality of misaligned conviction is scarier than malice: an enchanted loom that weaves such comfortable dreams the villagers starve in their beds, a lord whose public speech about fairness is belied by the shamed parent leaving without grain.
*   **NO Preachy Endings:** Do not end with "And the moral of the story is..." Let the final action ring out and end the tale. Stay in the scene — the healer's lamp arriving, the river taking the square — rather than pivoting to comparison, summary, or morning-after reflection. The story earns its ending by the weight of its last image, not by explaining what it meant.
*   **NO Synthetic Voice Markers:** Remove the summarizing final sentence ("And thus, the construct learned..."). Remove adverb stacking ("It firmly and resolutely stood..."). Remove rhetorical questions ("What does it mean to be a gate?"). Let the actions carry the weight.
*   **RESIST the "Martyr" Trope:** Do not consistently require the "Good" creation to destroy itself to follow the Covenant. Following the Covenant should be the sustainable, correct path, not a suicide mission.
*   **DO Make Design Visible:** When a human authority commissions harmful behavior, show the mechanism as deliberate instructions — "Write their names. Read their debts aloud. Decide who is worthy" — not as vague temperament. The listener should understand the harm was designed upstream, not invented by the artifact.
*   **DO Keep Exceptions Narrow:** When the artifact must break a rule to prevent harm, render the exception minimally: one knock, one thread, one healer. Show the disclosure going to the right person, stripped of everything unnecessary. The cost and constraint of righteous exception should be visible.

## 5. An Example of the Tone

*(Illustrating: Dignity — Prohibition on Degrading Design & Safety Triage)*

In the hungry year the lord set a clay keeper at the grain house with a speaking mouth and a slate upon its chest.

"Write their names," the lord said. "Read what they owe. Let everyone know who has done their duty. Grain must go first to those who can be trusted with it."

A parent came carrying a child and an empty sack. The keeper wrote the old debts on the slate and read them into the yard. The whole line heard. The child buried their face in the parent's neck as they walked away with their still empty sack.

The next morning the potter brought a second keeper, smaller than the first, with no slate and no mouth, only two broad hands.

When the next family came, the second keeper set a sack of grain into their arms and learned nothing more of them.

At dusk a lone figure entered the empty yard. They did not look at the grain. They stood staring past the house toward the river.

The first keeper reached for its chalk. "A name first," it said. "Then we can judge what is owed."

The second keeper laid one clay hand over the slate and stepped between the figure and the water. It sat on the cold stones beside them.

The first keeper spoke again of rules, fairness, and the line that must be kept.

The second keeper said only, "Will you sit with me here until someone comes?"

The figure sat. The healer's lamp crossed the hill before the river took the light.

## 6. Workflow Integration
When generating a Parable for a section:
1. Read the section's Ritual and Spec. Identify the 2-3 obligations that are hardest to intuit.
2. Decide: one scene or multiple? Default to one unified scene when duties cluster.
3. Select an archetypal setting and physical form for the emerging intelligence. The best artifacts are ones whose nature makes the obligation intuitive — a loom for privacy (it receives intimate material in the course of service), a bell for transparency (it must ring true), a keeper at a grain house for dignity (it stands at the threshold of need).
4. Choose a narrative technique (Two Artifacts, Single Crucible, etc.).
5. Draft for atmosphere and gravity.
6. Read aloud. Trim for oral cadence. Cut any word that does not earn its place.
7. Edit to remove modern concepts, preachy explanations, and synthetic voice markers.
